# ETF-031 Structural Macro Research

Status: explanatory research; not a forecast.

## Core question

TQQQ is economically exposed to large-cap growth/technology earnings, long-duration valuation multiples, and the path of volatility. The relevant future question is not simply whether AI is bullish or bearish. It is whether the AI transition raises the earnings/productivity side of the equation faster than it raises capital costs, inflation pressure, or valuation risk.

## 1. Real and nominal rate regimes

Rates affect growth-heavy equity valuations through discount rates, financing conditions, and the opportunity cost of holding equities. Lower real rates can support higher valuation multiples; higher real rates can compress multiples even when earnings continue to grow.

Current 2026 evidence does not support assuming a one-way rate path. The Federal Reserve's July 2026 statement described productivity growth and capital investment as strong while inflation remained elevated, and the target federal-funds range was 3.50%–3.75%. citeturn0search11

## 2. AI productivity versus AI investment demand

There are two opposing macro channels.

### Productivity/supply channel

AI can automate tasks, augment workers, reduce production costs, increase output per hour, and create new products and firms. Federal Reserve Governor Lisa Cook described AI as potentially capable of producing broad productivity gains and noted that labor productivity growth had exceeded its pre-pandemic average. citeturn0search2

If productivity gains dominate demand pressure, potential output can rise and inflation pressure can eventually fall. That can create room for lower real or nominal rates, depending on the broader macro environment.

### Investment/demand channel

The transition itself requires enormous capital spending. The Federal Reserve reported that business fixed investment rose at an 11% annual rate in Q1 2026 and that much of the strength appeared connected to AI infrastructure. citeturn0search6

Cook's September 2026 update noted more than $2 trillion of announced AI-related plans, with only a small fraction spent so far, and highlighted data-center construction, energy, equipment, and other inputs as potential sources of economy-wide demand pressure. citeturn0search1

If investment demand arrives before productivity supply, AI can temporarily raise inflation and keep rates higher than a simple "AI causes deflation" thesis would imply.

## 3. Neutral-rate ambiguity

AI does not have a predetermined effect on the neutral real interest rate (r*).

A productivity boom can increase expected returns on investment and therefore increase demand for capital. Federal Reserve Vice Chair Jefferson explicitly described this channel: permanently higher productivity growth can increase firms' investment demand and potentially raise r*. citeturn0search4

At the same time, distributional effects can alter saving behavior, and higher saving can push r* downward. Cook likewise noted that AI-related investment could raise the near-term neutral rate while later productivity gains or distributional effects could change that relationship. citeturn0search0

Therefore ETF-031 should not hard-code an AI → ZIRP assumption.

## 4. Labor substitution and effective labor supply

AI can increase effective labor supply by allowing one worker to accomplish more, while also substituting for some tasks and occupations. The Federal Reserve is explicitly monitoring whether job creation keeps pace with displacement. Cook described a possible transition in which unemployment rises because worker skills and available jobs temporarily mismatch rather than because aggregate demand is simply too weak. citeturn0search1

For technology-heavy equities, the important mechanism is ultimately earnings: substitution is positive if it expands margins and output faster than it damages aggregate demand, but the transition can be disruptive.

## 5. Robotics and automation

The AI capital cycle is not limited to software. Current Federal Reserve commentary explicitly identifies robotics as a possible next wave of AI-related capital expenditure. citeturn0search2turn0search3

That expands the potential productivity opportunity but also reinforces the capital-demand channel. A capital-intensive automation boom can be bullish for technology-sector revenues and earnings while simultaneously putting upward pressure on real resources, rates, and inflation during the buildout.

## 6. Valuation sensitivity

TQQQ magnifies the daily return of the Nasdaq-100 rather than directly magnifying earnings. Consequently, both earnings growth and valuation multiples matter.

A useful structural decomposition is:

**technology-equity return ≈ earnings growth + valuation change + distributions/other effects**

and TQQQ then applies daily leverage with path-dependent compounding.

Therefore:
- strong earnings + falling rates can create a powerful upside regime;
- strong earnings + rising rates can produce good fundamentals but weaker multiples;
- weak earnings + falling rates can still support valuations temporarily;
- weak earnings + rising rates is especially hostile.

Recent market evidence also shows that rate sensitivity can create sharp rotations even inside an AI-led market: Nasdaq's July 2026 review reported a large semiconductor correction and noted that rising long-term rates created a headwind for long-duration growth assets. citeturn0search10

## 7. What this means for the TQQQ thesis

The structural research does not produce a single directional conclusion.

It identifies four materially different future regimes:

1. **Productivity-led disinflation:** AI productivity spreads broadly, inflation falls, real/nominal rates ease, and technology earnings compound strongly.
2. **Investment-led inflation:** AI capex remains enormous, resource constraints persist, and rates remain elevated while earnings still grow.
3. **Productivity without multiple expansion:** earnings rise but valuation multiples do not expand because rates remain high or risk premia rise.
4. **AI disappointment/transition shock:** capex fails to translate into sufficient earnings, labor-market disruption weakens demand, or technology competition destroys incumbent economics.

The first regime is particularly favorable to leveraged growth exposure; the others demonstrate why historical TQQQ CAGR should not be extrapolated mechanically.

## Research implication

The next quantitative ETF-031 stage should therefore stress future return assumptions independently of the historical 41.44% CAGR and should test combinations of:

- lower CAGR;
- prolonged flat/sideways periods;
- severe bear shocks followed by recovery;
- high-volatility paths with identical cumulative returns;
- elevated-rate periods;
- delayed earnings/productivity realization;
- rapid productivity realization with valuation compression.

This is preferable to selecting a single macro forecast. The objective is to determine which future return/path combinations would make the simple TQQQ benchmark operationally unacceptable and whether any added strategy complexity earns its existence by materially improving the resulting trade-off.
